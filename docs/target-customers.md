# Ownlane target customers

## Purpose

This is the working customer map for discovery interviews, early access, and launch decisions. It is deliberately broader than a single creator archetype, but it does not treat every person with an audience as an equally good early customer.

Ownlane is for people turning work, expertise, community, or taste into income online. The common thread is not their medium. It is the business problem: they have more than one way to earn or intend to, and those ways are scattered across tools that do not feel like one business.

## The highest-value early customer

The best first Ownlane customer is an independent creator with an existing audience or active distribution who:

- earns from, or is actively setting up, at least two offer types;
- already uses two or more separate tools for links, selling, booking, community, support, or email;
- cares that their storefront feels like their brand rather than a generic profile;
- wants direct customer and audience relationships;
- feels the cost of lost context, multiple checkouts, duplicated setup, or fragmented analytics;
- is willing to pay a monthly software subscription to look and operate more professionally.

The practical shorthand is: **a creator with a real business, not just a profile.**

## Priority 1: interview and design partners

These customers have the clearest problem-to-product fit. Recruit from every group, but do not build separate products for each one yet.

### 1. Digital-product educators

Examples: course creators, newsletter writers, template sellers, Notion creators, design educators, career educators, marketing educators, and niche experts.

- **They sell:** guides, courses, templates, toolkits, paid newsletters, workshops, memberships, and consulting.
- **Their current stack:** a link page, Gumroad or similar storefront, email software, a course tool, and sometimes Calendly.
- **What breaks:** their audience moves through multiple brands and checkouts; their customer data is split; a consulting lead may never see a relevant product.
- **Why Ownlane matters:** a single branded home makes products, sessions, memberships, and email capture feel like one business.
- **Interview signal:** they can name a recent launch, customer journey, or tool they wish they could remove.

### 2. AI-native makers

Examples: prompt engineers, vibe coders, automation builders, agent builders, AI workflow creators, and people teaching others how to use AI at work.

- **They sell:** prompt packs, workflow libraries, agent templates, code starters, automations, audits, implementation sessions, and memberships.
- **Their current stack:** X or LinkedIn, a link page, Gumroad or Lemon Squeezy, GitHub, Discord, and booking software.
- **What breaks:** their offers change quickly; the value is often spread across files, demos, content, and paid help; the storefront rarely explains the ecosystem well.
- **Why Ownlane matters:** a home built for both digital products and paid expertise, with an audience record that connects a buyer to future upgrades or help.
- **Interview signal:** they have sold or seriously considered selling a workflow, prompt, template, automation, or implementation service.

### 3. Productized experts

Examples: consultants, coaches, strategists, freelancers, operators, designers, developers, and fractional specialists.

- **They sell:** audits, strategy calls, retainers, fixed-scope services, implementation, digital resources, and workshops.
- **Their current stack:** personal site, Calendly, invoicing, Stripe links, Notion, LinkedIn, and a separate resource shop.
- **What breaks:** the buyer journey splits between credibility, booking, payment, and follow-up; lower-cost products do not naturally lead to higher-value services.
- **Why Ownlane matters:** their knowledge products and time-based offers live together, helping supporters become customers and customers become repeat buyers.
- **Interview signal:** they repeatedly answer the same question before a client books, or manually direct people among their site, calendar, and payment link.

### 4. Community-led creators

Examples: community builders, cohort leaders, paid Discord operators, local or global niche groups, and creators with recurring events.

- **They sell:** memberships, event access, workshops, community sponsorships, digital resources, and one-to-one sessions.
- **Their current stack:** Discord, Circle, Slack, Patreon, Eventbrite, Stripe, email software, and a link page.
- **What breaks:** people do not understand what is free, what is paid, or where to start; the organizer cannot see the full relationship with a member.
- **Why Ownlane matters:** a branded front door that makes joining, attending, buying, and supporting coherent.
- **Interview signal:** they run recurring events or memberships and can describe churn, event conversion, or onboarding friction.

### 5. Indie hackers and developer-creators

Examples: solo SaaS founders with a personal audience, open-source maintainers, developer educators, technical newsletter writers, and creators of developer tools.

- **They sell:** starter kits, boilerplates, components, APIs, templates, sponsorships, advisory calls, paid communities, and small software products.
- **Their current stack:** personal site, GitHub, Lemon Squeezy or Gumroad, Discord, X, an email tool, and Stripe.
- **What breaks:** product updates, educational content, community, and professional services are separate experiences; early supporters are hard to recognize and reward.
- **Why Ownlane matters:** it provides a commerce home around the maker, not only one SaaS product.
- **Interview signal:** their public identity and their products are already intertwined, but their web presence is not.

### 6. Creative professionals with multiple revenue streams

Examples: illustrators, photographers, designers, motion artists, musicians, writers, and video creators who do more than take commissions.

- **They sell:** commissions, prints, downloads, presets, sample packs, memberships, tips, licensing, workshops, and digital resources.
- **Their current stack:** Instagram or TikTok, a link page, Etsy or Shopify, Ko-fi or Patreon, a portfolio, and sometimes booking or email software.
- **What breaks:** their creative identity is fragmented into separate shops and support pages; supporters cannot easily see all the ways to buy or commission.
- **Why Ownlane matters:** a premium branded home that can hold art, commerce, services, and fan support without feeling transactional.
- **Interview signal:** they have both an audience-support model and a product, licensing, or client-work model.

## Priority 2: important adjacent customers

These audiences are in scope and should be interviewed after the Priority 1 groups reveal the common product core.

### 7. Video creators, podcasters, and streamers

They may sell sponsors, memberships, merch, digital products, live-event access, tips, coaching, and affiliate-led offers. The opportunity is real, but many have platform-native monetization already. Prioritize those with off-platform offers and a strong desire for a direct audience relationship.

### 8. Writers and niche media brands

They may sell paid newsletters, memberships, sponsorships, reports, workshops, archives, and community access. Ownlane is compelling when the publication is becoming a broader business rather than only a subscription.

### 9. Fitness, wellness, and skills instructors

They may sell programs, plans, sessions, challenges, subscriptions, events, and downloadable resources. The core need is a single journey from content to program to booking, with careful attention to claims, privacy, and scheduling requirements.

### 10. Speakers and public-facing founders

They may sell talks, advisory time, books, resources, private communities, events, and sponsorships. They are a good fit when their individual brand, rather than one company product, drives demand.

### 11. Curators and taste-led commerce creators

Examples: recommendation newsletters, lifestyle curators, collectors, and niche reviewers. They may sell guides, paid access, affiliate recommendations, collections, events, and their own products. They are valuable when Ownlane supports creator-owned offers first, not only affiliate links.

### 12. Micro media studios and creator duos

Small teams with a visible creator brand may need multiple editors, shared offers, memberships, and sponsorship operations. They fit the Business plan after the single-creator workflow is strong.

## Priority 3: later segments

These are possible customers but should not determine early product scope.

- Large creators with management teams and enterprise contracts
- Talent agencies and creator-management businesses
- Traditional ecommerce brands with no meaningful individual creator identity
- Marketplaces that need multi-vendor capabilities
- Universities, employers, and internal knowledge teams
- Charities and fundraisers with compliance-heavy donation needs
- Creators whose only need is a free link list

## Who is not an early target

Ownlane should politely avoid competing for customers whose primary need is:

- a free link-in-bio page with no plan to transact;
- a full ecommerce catalog, inventory management, or warehouse fulfilment system;
- a complex learning-management system with formal accreditation;
- enterprise CRM, agency client management, or marketplace operations;
- unlimited AI media generation at a low monthly price.

Serving these needs too early would dilute the creator-business-home promise.

## Discovery recruitment matrix

For the first 30 interviews, recruit a mix that tests the central hypothesis instead of only speaking to friends in one niche.

| Segment | Interviews | Required qualification |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Digital-product educators | 6 | At least one paid product and one other offer or channel |
| AI-native makers | 6 | Sells or intends to sell prompts, workflows, agents, code, or implementation |
| Productized experts | 6 | Sells time plus a repeatable resource or service package |
| Community-led creators | 4 | Runs membership, recurring events, or a paid group |
| Indie hackers and developer-creators | 4 | Has a public audience and at least one monetized offer |
| Creative professionals | 4 | Earns from a mix of creative work, products, support, or commissions |

Aim for a mix of early-stage and established earners, but do not recruit people who only like the idea. Recruit people who can show a real current workflow, recent sale, or point of friction.

## What to learn in interviews

Do not pitch Ownlane first. Start from the creators current reality.

1. Walk me through the last time someone paid you.
2. Where did that person first find you, and what did they do next?
3. What else could that same person have bought, booked, joined, or supported?
4. Which tools are involved from discovery through payment and follow-up?
5. Where do people drop off or get confused?
6. Which part of your business feels least like your brand?
7. What audience or customer information do you wish lived in one place?
8. What have you tried to consolidate already, and why did it not stick?
9. What would make moving away from your current stack worth the effort?
10. If you could change one thing about how people support your work, what would it be?

## Early positioning test

Test these two statements in interviews. Ask which is clearer, more valuable, and more believable.

> **Ownlane is the creator business home: one branded place to sell what you make, offer your time, and grow the people who support you.**

> **Ownlane turns scattered ways of earning into one creator-owned business.**

The first is clearer about the product. The second is stronger as a strategic promise. The goal is to learn whether the creator sees their own current situation in either statement.

## Notes after every conversation

Capture the same fields after each interview:

- Creator segment and audience size
- Revenue streams and approximate monthly range, if they are comfortable sharing
- Current tools and costs
- Existing custom domain or website
- Most painful handoff in the buyer journey
- Products or offer types they would move first
- Most valuable outcome in their own words
- Switching concern or deal breaker
- Willingness to join early access or pay for a solution

Patterns across these conversations should determine the launch wedge. The objective is not to validate that creators have many tools. It is to learn which fragmented journey Ownlane must solve noticeably better than any alternative.
